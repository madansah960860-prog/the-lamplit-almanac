A note for every day

The oldest almanacs marked the days of the year with the events, feasts, and phenomena worth remembering, and we keep that gentle tradition here. Each day, the box above shows an entry drawn from our collection for today's date. Below is the whole collection, a year's worth of moments that changed America and the world, so you may browse at leisure and find the days that matter to you.

Every entry is a well-documented event, checked against reliable references, and written in our own words. We have gathered at least two for every month, and we add to the collection over time. They range from the founding of nations to the flick of a switch that lit the first electric lamp — for history is made of great turning points and small, bright moments alike.

The almanac tradition

The almanac is one of the oldest and most beloved forms of the printed word. For centuries, families kept an almanac on the shelf beside the Bible, consulting it for the calendar, the phases of the moon, the tides, the weather to expect, and a daily note of what had happened on each date in years gone by. It was a humble, practical book, and also a companion — full of small wisdom, curiosities, and reminders of the turning year.

We keep a little of that spirit alive here, in the part of the almanac that recorded history day by day. There is a particular charm in knowing that this very date, across the centuries, has been a stage for courage, invention, and change. It connects the ordinary present to the great sweep of the past, and turns each day into a small anniversary of something worth remembering.

Why anniversaries matter

There is nothing magical about a date, and yet anniversaries hold real power over us. To learn that today marks the day a great document was signed, a frontier was crossed, or a brave stand was taken is to feel history draw a little nearer. The anniversary is a thread that ties us to those who came before, a reminder that the people of the past were as alive to their own moment as we are to ours.

Marking these days also helps us remember, in the oldest and simplest sense. A fact tied to a date on the calendar has a hook to hang on; it returns to us each year, a little deeper each time. This is why cultures the world over have kept anniversaries, feast days, and remembrances — they are how a people holds on to what matters. Our small collection is offered in that same spirit of remembering.

How we choose the entries

In selecting events for the almanac, we look for moments that are both well-documented and genuinely significant — turning points in the American story and the wider world, firsts that changed how people lived, and acts of courage that still inspire. We favor events whose dates are firmly settled, and we write each entry in our own words after checking it against reliable references.

We also aim for variety and fairness, gathering events from across the centuries and from many fields — government and invention, exploration and the long struggle for rights and freedom. History is not only the doings of the powerful, and we try to honor the quieter courage that shaped the world as much as the grand proclamations. The collection grows over time, and reader suggestions of well-documented events are always welcome.

Great anniversaries of the year

Some dates ring out across the centuries. The Fourth of July needs no introduction to any American; it marks the adoption of the Declaration of Independence in 1776, the birth certificate of the nation. The twentieth of July recalls the summer evening in 1969 when human beings first set foot upon the Moon, a feat that still beggars belief. The nineteenth of November holds Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, a few immortal sentences spoken over a battlefield that redefined the nation's purpose.

Other dates mark quieter but no less important turns. The eighteenth of August recalls the ratification, in 1920, of the amendment that at last secured women's right to vote. The first of December remembers the day in 1955 when Rosa Parks, refusing to surrender her bus seat, lit a spark that would help kindle the civil rights movement. Each of these days, returning faithfully every year, is an invitation to pause and remember how far courage and conviction have carried us.

Sharing the day with others

An anniversary is a gift best shared. Reading out today's entry at the breakfast table, mentioning it to a grandchild, or bringing it up with a friend turns a solitary fact into a shared moment and often a conversation. Children in particular delight in learning that something remarkable happened on an ordinary day, and the habit plants seeds of curiosity that may grow for a lifetime.

Many families and classrooms have long kept a "this day in history" ritual, and it is an easy and rewarding one to adopt. It costs nothing, takes only a moment, and gives everyone a small thread of connection to the great human story. We hope our collection makes it easy, and that you will find in it, day by day, something worth passing on.

Keeping memory alive

There is a deeper purpose to an "on this day" collection than mere curiosity, and it is this: the keeping of memory. A people is held together in no small part by what it chooses to remember, and the great events of the past survive only so long as each generation troubles to pass them on. When we pause on an anniversary to recall a brave act or a hard-won freedom, we are doing the quiet, essential work of remembering, so that what was gained is not forgotten and what was suffered is not repeated.

This is especially true of the harder chapters of history. It is comfortable to recall the triumphs and look away from the injustices, but an honest almanac does neither. The courage of those who struggled against slavery, or for the vote, or for the simple dignity of a bus seat, deserves to be marked as faithfully as any victory or invention. In remembering the whole of our history, the shadows as well as the light, we honor those who came before and grow a little wiser about our own time. That is memory doing its truest work.

A daily habit worth keeping

There is something steadying about beginning the day with a glance at history — a reminder that this very date, across the centuries, has seen courage and invention and change. Many readers enjoy checking the almanac each morning as they might check the weather, gathering a fact or two to carry through the day and share at the table. It asks only a moment, and it leaves you a little richer than it found you — a small, dependable pleasure to set beside the morning coffee and the day's first look at the sky.

If a date you care about is missing, or you spot an error in one of our entries, we should be glad to hear from you; the almanac grows and improves by its readers' help. Meanwhile, the longer stories behind many of these dates wait in our American History and World History shelves, and the people behind them in our Biographies.
